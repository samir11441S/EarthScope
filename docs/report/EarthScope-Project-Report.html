Three findings to remember

  1. Many “trends” in raw data near Bangladesh are partly artefacts. 224 of 693 NASA POWER grid cells around Bangladesh contain a non-climatic step (most often 2008). Without break testing, 21% of the area would look significantly cooling; with it, 8% — and 36% significantly warming.
  2. Dhaka is warming, but raw NASA POWER hides it. POWER shows a false 1997 drop; the independent ERA5 dataset confirms it is an artefact and shows +0.21 °C per decade (1981–2025).
  3. One driver, opposite trends. Around Dhaka, every March since 2001, irrigated fields cool by day (−0.58 °C/decade) yet warm by night (+0.41 °C/decade) and green up strongly (79% of pixels significant), while the city–countryside contrast grows by +0.55 °C/decade.

4The problem and our answer

The problem

Bangladesh is among the countries most exposed to climate change: heat waves, monsoon floods, cyclones and storm surges, sea-level rise on a sinking delta, and some of the worst air in the world. The data to understand these risks exist — NASA and other agencies publish them openly — but they are scattered across dozens of portals, in technical formats, in English, and often with traps (data breaks, colour-coded images, uncertain trends) that lead non-experts to wrong conclusions.

Our answer

Collect20+ open sources, fetched live by our server and cached sensibly.

→

→
ActSafety checks, shelters, alerts, offline pack, reports to share with decision-makers.

Challenge fit

EarthScope uses NASA Earth-science data as its core (NASA POWER, GIBS imagery from MODIS, VIIRS, Landsat/HLS, GPM, EONET events, GISTEMP, SEDAC population, LANCE flood product) and adds partner data where NASA has no product for the job (ECMWF ERA5 for validation, Copernicus CAMS for air quality, IPCC AR6 for sea level, OpenStreetMap for shelters).

Design principles we kept throughout. (1) Every number shows its source. (2) Never call a trend real unless it is statistically significant. (3) Never show a scenario as a forecast. (4) Never present EarthScope as an official warning service — always point to BMD/DDM and 999. (5) It must work in Bangla and on a cheap phone.

6The science: how every number is made

6.1 Detecting data breaks (homogeneity)

NASA POWER is a reanalysis (MERRA-2): a weather model constantly corrected by observations. When the set of observations changes (new satellites, stations lost), the series can jump. Over Bangladesh, POWER temperature drops about 1 °C in 1997 and rainfall jumps again around 2016 — not real climate.

Method: two-phase regression with a common slope (Lund & Reeves 2002). For each possible break year we fit “trend” vs “trend + step” and compute an F statistic; the maximum F is compared with 99% critical values we computed by Monte-Carlo simulation (20,000 random series per length). A break must also be large enough to matter (≥ 0.3 °C, or ≥ 15% of mean rainfall). Years before a break are shown grey and excluded from every trend. Code: lib/homogeneity.ts, lib/climate.ts.

6.2 Independent cross-check with ERA5

ERA5 (ECMWF / Copernicus) is a separate reanalysis with a different model and assimilation system. For each place EarthScope downloads ERA5 daily data (1981–2025), makes annual values and compares them with POWER: year-to-year correlation, average bias, trends over the same years, rainfall ratio. Most importantly it runs the break test on the difference series POWER − ERA5: a step there proves the break belongs to one dataset, not to the climate (the “reference series” method of relative homogenisation). Code: lib/crosscheck.ts, /api/crosscheck.

6.3 Honest trend statistics

  • Trend = least-squares slope, reported per decade, with a 95% confidence interval.
  • Significance from a t-test corrected for autocorrelation (effective sample size, Santer et al. 2000) — climate years are not independent, and ignoring this makes trends look more certain than they are.
  • The words “warming” or “cooling” appear only when p < 0.05; otherwise EarthScope says “no clear trend yet”. Reports use IPCC calibrated likelihood language.
  • Decade comparisons need ≥ 20 consistent years. Code: lib/stats.ts.

6.4 Climate extremes

From NASA POWER daily data, following the WMO ETCCDI index conventions with fixed thresholds: hot days (Tmax ≥ 35 °C), warm nights (Tmin ≥ 25 °C), humid-heat days (wet-bulb ≥ 27 °C), frost days, hottest day, heavy-rain days (≥ 50 mm), wettest day, longest dry spell. Computed only over the break-free years. Code: lib/extremes.ts.

6.5 Decoding satellite maps exactly

NASA GIBS serves measured products as coloured images using fixed published colour maps. EarthScope downloads the PNG images and converts each pixel back to its value using those colour maps:

  • Land cover (MODIS MCD12Q1): 17 IGBP classes, each a unique colour → grouped into built-up, cropland, forest, shrub/grass, wetland, water, barren.
  • NDVI (MOD13Q1): a 250-step palette → vegetation index.
  • Land-surface temperature (MOD11A2): 250 colours in 0.6 K steps from −73 °C to +77 °C. In testing, all 65,536 pixels of a Dhaka tile decoded exactly.

Comparisons always use the same product and the same season in both years, so a difference reflects the land, not the sensor. Code: lib/earth-change.ts.

6.6 Surface heat: change relative to the surroundings

One April can be hotter than another everywhere because of weather. So besides the area-mean change, EarthScope removes the area-wide shift and marks pixels that warmed or cooled ≥ 1.5 °C more than the area as a whole — typically new buildings and lost trees (warmer) or new water and parks (cooler). It also reports hot-spot intensity (mean of the hottest 10% minus the median), a standard surface urban-heat-island measure.

6.7 Air quality and sea level

  • Air quality: Copernicus CAMS hourly forecast (PM2.5, PM10, ozone, NO₂) via Open-Meteo; US EPA AQI categories with official health advice; PM2.5 compared with the WHO 2021 guideline (15 µg/m³ daily). Code: lib/air-quality.ts.
  • Sea level: IPCC AR6 WGI Table 9.9 global-mean projections (e.g. SSP2-4.5 in 2100: 0.56 m, likely 0.44–0.76 m) and observed rise since 1901 (≈0.20 m, now ≈3.7 mm/yr). Ground height from Copernicus GLO-90. Code: lib/sea-level.ts.

6.8 Daily real clouds on the globe

The server downloads the NOAA-20 and Suomi-NPP true-colour images of a day plus the previous 6 days. Clouds move but ice, snow and deserts don't, so the darkest value of each pixel over the past days is the ground; only what is brighter (and white) on the chosen day is cloud. Taking the darker of the two satellite passes removes ocean sun-glint. Built in about 8 seconds, cached for a year per date, pre-built every night by a scheduled job. Code: lib/clouds.ts, /api/clouds.

6.9 Hazard zones and safety

Impact zones are distance estimates from magnitude, wind speed or alert level (or measured footprints where GDACS provides them); cyclone positions along forecast tracks are interpolated in time. Shelters, hospitals and assembly points come from OpenStreetMap; any inside a hazard zone are hidden. This is a screening aid, never an official warning. Code: lib/hazards.ts, lib/safe-places.ts.

6.10 Trend Lab statistics (maps of many places)

  • Mann–Kendall test: compares every pair of years and counts how often the later value is higher. Rank-based, so it is robust to outliers and to skewed variables such as rainfall. Includes the tie correction.
  • Trend-free pre-whitening (Yue et al. 2002): remove the trend, measure lag-1 autocorrelation, remove it if significant, add the trend back, then test — so persistent year-to-year memory is not mistaken for a trend.
  • Sen's slope: the median of all pairwise slopes — the standard robust “how much” paired with Mann–Kendall. Reported per decade.
  • False-discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg, α = 10%, as recommended by Wilks 2016 for climate maps): with hundreds of cells, ~5% would look significant by chance at p < 0.05. FDR keeps the expected share of false “significant” cells below 10%.
  • Per-cell break test for NASA POWER: the same Lund & Reeves test as for single places; if a step is found, only the years after it are tested (cells with fewer than 15 consistent years are excluded and shown grey).
  • Seasons: monthly values are combined into a seasonal total (rainfall) or day-weighted mean; winter is assigned to the year of its January.
  • Satellite pixels: MODIS monthly products decoded exactly from GIBS colour maps; land cover from MODIS MCD12Q1 (2001 and 2024) labels each pixel.

Code: lib/trends.ts, lib/trend-lab.ts, components/trend-lab.tsx, /api/trend-grid; tests in tests/trends.test.ts (including a textbook FDR example and a planted-step case).

7Our key findings

7.1 Dhaka: the hidden warming

This is the strongest story to tell judges: it shows why careful data handling matters.

QuestionNaive answer (raw NASA POWER)EarthScope's answer
Is Dhaka warming?Full record 1981–2025 shows cooling — because of the artificial 1997 drop.The 1997 step is detected (≈ −1 °C) and excluded. POWER after 1997 shows no clear trend (0.00 °C/decade).
Is the 1997 drop real?Cannot tell.No. The POWER − ERA5 difference series itself jumps −1.0 °C in 1997–98: the fault is in POWER.
So what is the real trend?—ERA5 has no break and shows +0.21 °C per decade over 1981–2025 (+0.24 since 1997). Agreement POWER vs ERA5 after 1997 is weak (r = 0.47), so EarthScope shows the cross-check openly.
How has the land changed?—Built-up share of the 45 km Dhaka square: 13.9% (2001) → 19.5% (2024), about 109 km² more. Outskirts warmed more than their surroundings (surface heat change map).
Is the air safe?—On test days the US AQI was 151–168 (“Unhealthy”), PM2.5 ≈ 6× the WHO daily guideline.
Sea level?—Central Dhaka ≈ 19 m above sea level → not directly flooded by the sea, but backwater, waterlogging and salinity reach inland; the delta is sinking several mm/year in many places.
How to say it. “Raw NASA data would tell Dhaka it is cooling. EarthScope found a 1997 fault in the data, proved it with a second, independent dataset, and shows the real picture: about +0.2 °C per decade of warming, a city that grew by over 100 km² of concrete, and air that is unhealthy on many days.”

Numbers were produced by the live app on 3 October 2026; air quality and hazards change daily.

7.2 Trend detective: how much of the map is real?

Trend Lab, NASA POWER, Bangladesh and neighbours (20–30°N, 80–100°E, 693 cells), 1981–2025, FDR 10%:

Variable · seasonNaive analysis (no break test)EarthScope (break-tested)
Air temperature · whole year39% rising · 21% falling36% rising · 8% falling · 224 cells had a step (most often 2008)
Rainfall · monsoon (Jun–Sep)69% rising · 0% falling50% rising · 1% falling · 287 cells had a step
What this means. Without break testing, the share of the region that appears to be cooling is 2.6 times too large, and monsoon rainfall looks wetter almost everywhere. Even after break handling, the widespread POWER rainfall increase should be treated with caution (a second break or changing inputs can remain) — which is exactly why EarthScope shows the test, the breaks and the ERA5 cross-check instead of a single confident number.

The temperature map also shows opposite signs inside one region: warming over the Himalayan foothills, eastern Bangladesh and Myanmar, and a cooling pocket over parts of the Ganges plain — a pattern often linked in research to aerosol haze and irrigation (a regional “warming hole”). EarthScope flags it; confirming the cause would need further study.

7.3 One driver, opposite trends — irrigation around Dhaka

Trend Lab case study, NASA MODIS Terra, 240 km around Dhaka, every March 2001–2026, mean Sen's slope per decade by MODIS land cover; % = share of pixels with a significant trend (FDR 10%):

NASA variable (March)Built-up landCroplandReading
Daytime surface temperature−0.03 °C/decade−0.58 °C/decade (33% sig.)Fields cool by day; city–field contrast grows +0.55 °C/decade
Night-time surface temperature+0.41 °C/decade+0.41 °C/decade (19% sig.)The same fields warm at night — opposite to daytime
Vegetation greenness (NDVI)−0.007/decade+0.047/decade (79% sig.)Fields green up (more irrigation); the city browns
The mechanism. Dry-season boro rice irrigation has spread across the delta. Water evaporating from greener fields uses the Sun's energy, cooling the ground by day; at night there is no sunshine to drive evaporation, and wet soil and humid air hold heat, so the same fields warm. The city does not cool like its surroundings, so the surface urban heat island grows. Same process, opposite trends — by time of day and by land type. This matches published research on irrigated regions.

12Bangla (বাংলা) and accessibility

  • Whole interface in Bangla: a switch at the top (EN | বাংলা), remembered per browser and chosen automatically for Bangla-language browsers.

  • Safety net: npm run i18n:check lists untranslated strings; the test suite fails if any is missing or if a translation drops a {placeholder}.
  • The climate story, reports (all three editions) and Ask Earth answers are also in Bangla.
  • Accessibility: keyboard-operable sliders (arrow keys, Home/End), screen-reader labels on controls, sufficient colour contrast, works on phones (tested at 375 px width).
Adding a new sentence? Write it in English inside t("…"), run npm run i18n:check -- --missing, add the Bangla line to lib/i18n-bn.ts, run npm test.

17Honest limitations

Know these before presenting — judges respect teams that state their limits.

LimitationWhat it means / what we do about it
Reanalysis, not stationsNASA POWER and ERA5 are ~50 km / ~25 km grid estimates, not Dhaka weather-station readings. BMD station data would be the next step (not openly available).
POWER and ERA5 disagree after 1997 in DhakaCorrelation is only 0.47; we show both and explain it rather than hide it.
Scenarios are not projectionsThe 2050 sliders are a simple sensitivity test, labelled as such; sea-level numbers are IPCC global means.
Hazard zones are estimatesDistance-based screening, not official warnings; shelters depend on OpenStreetMap volunteers' mapping.
Land-cover accuracyMODIS land cover is ~74% accurate overall; changes under 1–2 points may be noise; Bangladesh rural land is often labelled “savanna”.
Satellite resolutionLand cover 500 m, LST 1 km, NDVI 250 m — good for city trends, not individual buildings (the 30 m view is visual only).
Surface vs air temperatureLST is the temperature of roofs and ground at ~10:30, hotter than air; clouds leave gaps.
POWER rainfall trendsEven after break handling, POWER rainfall rises almost everywhere around Bangladesh; treat it with caution and compare with ERA5 or GPM.
MODIS Terra orbit driftSince about 2020 Terra's overpass time has been drifting earlier, which can slightly cool recent daytime surface temperatures. It affects city and fields alike, so the city–field contrast is robust; absolute daytime trends should be cross-checked with Aqua.
Field-significance choiceFDR at 10% is a standard choice for climate maps; a stricter level shows fewer significant cells. The map and the method are shown so experts can judge.
AI answersGrounded and sourced, but can still be wrong; we say so in the chat.
Free servicesDepends on free public APIs; caching, retries and fallbacks reduce but cannot remove outages.

Likely questions

Why Mann–Kendall and not just linear regression?It is rank-based, so robust to outliers and non-normal data like rainfall; it is the standard test in climate trend studies. We still show regression with confidence intervals for single places.
What is the false-discovery rate and why use it?Testing ~700 cells at p < 0.05 would mark ~35 as “significant” by pure chance. FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg, 10%) controls the share of false discoveries across the whole map — the approach recommended for climate maps (Wilks 2016).
How do you handle autocorrelation?Trend-free pre-whitening before Mann–Kendall; for single places, an effective-sample-size correction (Santer et al. 2000).
How do you know the 1997 / 2008 steps are fake?A statistical break test finds them, they appear in hundreds of cells at once, and the independent ERA5 dataset doesn't have them: the POWER − ERA5 difference jumps exactly then.
Is the irrigation story real or a coincidence?Three independent NASA measurements agree with one physical mechanism (day cooling, night warming, greening on cropland), the result is computed live and is significant in a third to four-fifths of cropland pixels after FDR, and it matches published research on irrigated regions.
Why not use weather stations?BMD station data are not openly available; we use two independent global datasets and state the limit.
Is this an official warning system?No — it is educational. It always links to BMD, FFWC, DDM and 999/1090.
Can it work outside Bangladesh?Yes — Trend Lab regions and pixel analyses work for any place; Bangladesh extras switch on automatically there.

20Glossary

AnomalyDifference from a reference period (e.g. +0.5 °C warmer than the first decade).
AQIAir Quality Index (US EPA scale 0–500); above 150 is “Unhealthy”.
AutocorrelationNeighbouring years resemble each other, so there is less independent information than the number of years suggests.
Break / inhomogeneityAn artificial jump in a data series caused by a change in how it was measured.
CAMSCopernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (EU air-quality forecasts).
Confidence interval (95%)The range that contains the true trend with 95% confidence.
ERA5ECMWF's global reanalysis; used here to cross-check NASA POWER.
ETCCDIWMO expert team that defines standard climate-extreme indices.
FDR (false-discovery rate)Method that controls how many “significant” results on a map are false alarms when many places are tested at once.
GIBSNASA Global Imagery Browse Services — serves satellite imagery and products.
Heat index“Feels-like” temperature combining heat and humidity.
HLSHarmonized Landsat Sentinel-2: 30 m imagery made comparable across satellites.
IGBPThe 17-class land-cover scheme used by MODIS.
IPCC AR6 / SSPThe UN climate panel's 6th assessment; SSPs are emission pathways (low to very high).
LSTLand-surface temperature: how hot the ground and roofs are.
Mann–Kendall testRank-based test for a consistent upward or downward trend.
MERRA-2NASA's reanalysis behind NASA POWER.
MODIS / VIIRSNASA satellite instruments imaging the whole Earth daily.
NDVIVegetation index from satellite light (higher = greener).
p-valueChance of seeing a trend this strong if there were none; below 0.05 counts as significant.
ReanalysisA weather model continuously corrected by observations, giving complete global data.
Sen's slopeMedian of all pairwise slopes — a robust “how much per year”.
Surface urban heat islandCities' ground surfaces being hotter than their surroundings.
